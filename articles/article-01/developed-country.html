WHAT DOES “DEVELOPED” REALLY MEAN IN THE 21st CENTURY?

I am not an economist. I am not a policymaker. I am just an ordinary person.

But sometimes an ordinary person asks a very simple question:

What is the use of being economically powerful if the basic systems that keep people safe can still fail?

I recently saw images of streets under water, public transport systems struggling, and rain entering underground infrastructure in one of the world's most economically powerful nations.

I did not feel satisfied seeing it.

I did not feel superior either.

Instead, I felt strangely uncomfortable.

Because suddenly, the distance between a so-called developed country and a so-called developing country did not look as large as the numbers suggested.

And that made me think.

Perhaps we have been measuring development through the wrong window.

WE HAVE BECOME VERY GOOD AT MEASURING WEALTH

For decades, development has been associated with economic power.

A larger economy.
Higher income.
More industries.
Advanced technology.
Modern infrastructure.
Financial markets.
Global corporations.
Skyscrapers.
High-speed transport.
Digital systems.

These are undoubtedly achievements.

But perhaps they answer only one question:

How powerful has a country become?

They do not necessarily answer another, more important question:

How resilient has a country become?

There is a difference.

A system can be enormously powerful and still be surprisingly vulnerable.

A country can produce extraordinary wealth and still struggle when extreme weather arrives.

A city can have intelligent buildings and sophisticated transportation systems and still be brought to a standstill when a basic infrastructure system fails.

A nation can possess advanced technology and still depend heavily on distant sources for essential goods.

So perhaps the definition of development needs another dimension.

DEVELOPMENT SHOULD NOT ONLY BE ABOUT HOW HIGH WE CAN RISE

Perhaps we should also ask:

How safely can we continue?

Imagine two countries.

One has a gigantic economy, advanced technology and enormous financial resources.

The other has a much smaller economy but has built strong local systems for food, water, energy, emergency response, essential manufacturing and community resilience.

Which one is more developed?

The answer is not obvious anymore.

Because wealth is an asset.

But resilience is a capability.

And when a crisis arrives, capability becomes visible.

WHAT ARE THE ESSENTIALS?

Strip civilisation down to its foundations.

Before stock markets, there is food.
Before financial instruments, there is water.
Before artificial intelligence, there is electricity.
Before global trade, there is local production.
Before smart cities, there must be safe cities.
Before economic growth, there must be human survival.

A truly developed society should therefore be able to continuously secure its essentials:

Water.
Food.
Energy.
Shelter.
Healthcare.
Transport.
Communication.
Waste management.
Essential manufacturing.
Emergency response.
Ecological security.

Not necessarily by producing everything itself.

But by ensuring that the systems supporting these essentials are secure, diversified, resilient and capable of recovering when disrupted.

That is a different definition of self-reliance.

It does not mean isolation.

It means not being helpless.

SELF-RELIANCE IS NOT THE OPPOSITE OF GLOBALISATION

A country does not need to manufacture every component, grow every crop or produce every resource within its borders.

The world is interconnected.

That interconnectedness has enormous value.

But interdependence without resilience can become vulnerability.

If one interruption can bring an essential system to its knees, perhaps the system was efficient — but not resilient.

Maybe the question for the 21st century is therefore not:

“Can we produce everything ourselves?”

It is:

“If something goes wrong somewhere, can we still continue?”

That is a much more practical definition of self-reliance.

THE NEXT DEVELOPMENT RACE MAY BE ABOUT RESILIENCE

The 20th century rewarded scale.

The 21st century may reward resilience.

We built enormous centralised systems because they were efficient.
We connected economies because global trade reduced costs.
We concentrated production because scale improved productivity.
We digitised everything because information became instantaneous.

All of this created extraordinary progress.

But every system also creates dependencies.

And dependencies create questions.

What happens when climate extremes become more frequent?
What happens when supply chains are interrupted?
What happens when energy systems are stressed?
What happens when water becomes scarce?
What happens when food production is disrupted?
What happens when cities grow faster than their infrastructure?
What happens when technology fails?

The developed country of tomorrow may therefore not be the one that simply builds the biggest systems.

It may be the one that builds systems capable of absorbing shocks without collapsing.

GDP CANNOT TELL THE WHOLE STORY

GDP can tell us how much economic activity is happening.

It cannot tell us everything about how securely people live.

A country can become richer while its citizens become more vulnerable to certain systemic failures.

A city can become wealthier while becoming more dependent on distant food, energy and water systems.

A company can become more profitable while becoming dangerously dependent on a single supplier.

An organisation can become more efficient while losing its ability to respond when conditions change.

This is why perhaps we need a second scoreboard.

Not instead of economic indicators.

Alongside them.

A Continuity Scorecard.

How secure is the water supply?
How resilient is food?
How diversified is energy?
How reliable is essential infrastructure?
How quickly can systems recover?
How dependent are critical services on single points of failure?
How prepared are communities for extreme events?
How much natural capital is being preserved?
How capable are local systems of responding when global systems are disrupted?

These questions may not look glamorous.

But when a crisis arrives, they suddenly become more important than glamour.

THE POOR PERSON MAY HAVE A DIFFERENT DEFINITION OF DEVELOPMENT

This is where I return to my first sentence.

I am just an ordinary person.

I don't look at a country through GDP tables every morning.

I look at it differently.

If my family has food, I call that security.
If there is clean water, I call that security.
If electricity continues during difficult times, I call that security.
If roads remain usable, hospitals remain functional and emergency services can respond, I call that security.
If my children can study, find meaningful work and breathe clean air, I call that progress.

Perhaps an ordinary person does not need a country to be the richest country on Earth.

Perhaps he simply wants the system around him to work when he needs it most.

That is not a sophisticated economic theory.

It is a human expectation.

MAYBE “DEVELOPED” NEEDS A NEW DEFINITION

Perhaps a developed country should be defined by two dimensions.

ECONOMIC STRENGTH
How much wealth, productivity, innovation and economic opportunity can it generate?

And:

SYSTEMIC RESILIENCE
How effectively can it protect essential human needs and continue functioning when the unexpected happens?

The first tells us how much a country can achieve.

The second tells us how much of that achievement can survive a crisis.

Together, they tell us something much closer to the real picture.

THE QUESTION IS NOT WHO IS DEVELOPED

Perhaps we should stop asking:

“Which country is developed?”

And start asking:

“Which systems are developed enough for the challenges ahead?”

Because climate change does not care about GDP rankings.
A flood does not check a country's economic classification.
A drought does not ask whether a nation is developed or developing.
A supply-chain disruption does not respect borders.
A power failure does not distinguish between rich and poor.

Nature has its own scoreboard.

And perhaps the 21st century is going to make us read it more carefully.

DEVELOPMENT AFTER THE AGE OF ABUNDANCE

For a long time, development meant having more.

More roads.
More buildings.
More cars.
More factories.
More consumption.
More technology.
More production.

But perhaps the next stage of civilisation will require a different question:

Can we have enough — securely, responsibly and continuously?

That changes the meaning of progress.

The goal is no longer simply to maximise production.

It is to create systems that can provide essential human needs without continuously creating new vulnerabilities.

That is a much harder challenge.

And perhaps that is precisely why it matters.

A DIFFERENT MEASURE OF PROGRESS

Maybe the developed country of the 21st century will not be the country with the tallest buildings.
Nor the biggest economy.
Nor the most expensive technology.
Nor the largest corporations.

Perhaps it will be the country that can say:

Our people will have water.
Our people will have food.
Our people will have energy.
Our essential systems will continue during disruption.
Our cities can withstand shocks.
Our economy can recover.
Our children can inherit a functioning future.

And perhaps the greatest achievement will be something that does not appear in a financial statement at all:

Continuity.

Because development should not merely make civilisation bigger.

It should make civilisation harder to break.

THE QUESTION WE SHOULD LEAVE BEHIND

So I return to my simple question.

What does “developed” really mean in the 21st century?

Perhaps it is not simply the ability to accumulate enormous wealth.

Perhaps it is the ability to convert wealth, knowledge and technology into security, resilience, dignity and continuity for ordinary people.

And perhaps one day, when we compare nations, we will look beyond:

How much do you produce?
and ask:
How well can you protect what matters?

Beyond:
How fast can you grow?
and ask:
How long can you continue?

Beyond:
How powerful are you?
and ask:
How prepared are you for a world that will not always behave as expected?

Because ultimately, development is not a race to become the richest.

It is a responsibility to become capable of carrying civilisation safely into the future.

And if that is the definition we choose,

perhaps the real question is no longer:

“Are we developed?”

It is:

“Are we developing in the right direction?”

"Adaptation is neither an accident nor an afterthought—it is a continuous negotiation between structure and circumstance."
